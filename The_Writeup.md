1. Executive Summary

For Non-Technical Stakeholders

Modern organizations increasingly rely on cloud identities, collaboration platforms, authentication workflows, and trusted communication channels.

This creates an important security reality:

«"An attacker does not always need to defeat a technical control directly. Sometimes, the more valuable target is the trust surrounding that control."»

Account Pressure is an authorized Red Team adversary simulation designed to examine how an organization could respond when an adversary attempts to create pressure around a user's identity and normal business workflow.

The objective was not to compromise real users, obtain credentials, or gain unauthorized access.

Instead, the assessment evaluated whether existing controls and processes could:

- Recognize suspicious identity-related activity.
- Detect abnormal authentication or authorization behavior.
- Identify unusual interaction patterns.
- Correlate events across available security telemetry.
- Distinguish suspicious behavior from legitimate business activity.
- Escalate potential incidents appropriately.
- Educate users without creating unnecessary operational disruption.
- Contain simulated activity before it could progress.

The assessment therefore treats security as a combination of:

«People + Identity + Technology + Detection + Response»

rather than evaluating a single security product in isolation.

---

2. Assessment Objective

The primary objective was to simulate a realistic identity-focused adversary scenario while maintaining strict authorization and safety boundaries.

The assessment examined three fundamental questions.

1. Could the organization recognize the behavior?

Can security controls distinguish suspicious activity from legitimate business activity?

2. Could the organization understand the behavior?

Once individual events are detected, can analysts correlate them into a meaningful attack narrative?

3. Could the organization respond effectively?

Can the organization contain simulated activity while minimizing disruption to legitimate users?

The assessment was therefore designed around the complete defensive lifecycle:

«Recognize → Correlate → Investigate → Respond → Improve»

3. Threat Model

The simulated adversary was modeled as an external threat actor attempting to exploit trust and account-related workflows rather than relying exclusively on direct technical exploitation.

The scenario considered common organizational dependencies such as:

- Cloud identity providers
- Collaboration platforms
- Authentication workflows
- Authorization requests
- Account notifications
- Security-related communications
- User decision-making
- Security monitoring

Trust Chaining

The central concept was trust chaining.

A user may trust:

1. Their organization.
2. Their organization's communication channels.
3. Their account notifications.
4. Their authentication process.
5. A familiar business workflow.

An adversary attempting to abuse this chain does not necessarily need to make every individual component malicious.

Instead, the adversary may attempt to make the overall interaction appear sufficiently legitimate.

This creates a security challenge in which individual components may appear normal while the combined sequence becomes suspicious.

«A trustworthy component does not automatically make the entire interaction trustworthy.»

---

4. Rules of Engagement

The simulation was conducted under a controlled authorization model.

Scope

Only explicitly approved systems, identities, workflows, and test scenarios were considered in scope.

Safety Boundaries

The exercise avoided:

- Real credential collection.
- Unauthorized access.
- Persistence on real systems.
- Malware deployment.
- Destructive actions.
- Real financial activity.
- Data exfiltration.
- Interaction with unrelated third-party systems.

All simulated activity remained within the defined assessment boundaries.

Success Condition

Success was not defined as "getting access."

Instead, success was measured by the amount of useful security information generated by the exercise.

This distinction is important in professional Red Teaming.

A controlled assessment should produce:

«Evidence → Findings → Defensive Improvements»

rather than simply demonstrating that an attack technique is technically possible.

5. Attack Simulation Concept

The scenario was built around a fictional account-security situation.

The simulated adversary attempted to create a believable sequence of events surrounding an identity-related workflow.

The scenario intentionally introduced elements that could cause a user or analyst to ask:

«"Is this a legitimate account event, or is someone attempting to manipulate the workflow?"»

The exercise therefore focused heavily on behavioral ambiguity.

Instead of depending on a single obvious malicious indicator, the assessment examined whether multiple weak signals could collectively reveal suspicious activity.

The underlying assumption was:

«"A suspicious event may not look suspicious in isolation. The sequence may be what makes it significant."»

---

6. Attack Lifecycle

The simulation was divided into several conceptual phases.

Phase 1 — Reconnaissance Modeling

The first stage focused on understanding the hypothetical target environment from an organizational perspective.

Questions included:

- Which identity systems are central to the organization?
- Which workflows are considered normal?
- Which communication channels are trusted?
- What events normally generate security notifications?
- Which telemetry sources would be available to defenders?

No unauthorized reconnaissance was performed.

The purpose of this phase was to model the attack surface from the perspective of identity, trust, and workflow dependencies.

---

Phase 2 — Trust Modeling

The next stage mapped the trust relationships surrounding the account.

A simplified model was:

User
  ↓
Corporate Identity
  ↓
Cloud Service
  ↓
Authentication / Authorization Workflow
  ↓
Security Monitoring

The objective was to identify where trust could become a security weakness.

This phase is important because the security of an identity workflow depends not only on authentication mechanisms, but also on the assumptions users and defenders make about the surrounding process.

---

Phase 3 — Controlled Simulation

A pre-authorized simulation was introduced into the defined environment.

The scenario was intentionally designed to resemble a plausible business or security-related interaction without collecting real credentials or performing unauthorized access.

The objective was to evaluate:

- User interaction.
- Security-control visibility.
- Alert generation.
- Analyst awareness.
- Workflow escalation.
- Response timing.
- Evidence available to defenders.

9. Technical Perspective

Identity Security

Modern identity systems create an important security challenge.

Authentication establishes that a credential, device, session, or authentication factor satisfied a particular requirement.

However:

«"Successful authentication does not automatically prove that the surrounding activity is trustworthy."»

A mature security architecture should therefore evaluate additional context, including:

- Who is performing the action?
- From where?
- Using which device?
- At what time?
- What normally happens for this account?
- What changed?
- What happened immediately before the event?
- What happened immediately after it?

This represents a transition from:

Authentication

to:

Context-Aware / Risk-Aware Authentication

10. Telemetry Correlation

One of the central concepts of the exercise was event correlation.

A single event may appear harmless when examined independently.

For example:

Event A — Successful Authentication

Individually, this may be completely legitimate.

However, security significance can change when the event is considered alongside other observations.

For example:

Event A — Authentication Event
        +
Event B — Account-Related Communication
        +
Event C — Unusual User Interaction
        +
Event D — Subsequent Redirect / Workflow Change

The individual events may not provide enough context on their own.

When correlated chronologically, however, they may form a pattern that warrants investigation.

The assessment therefore emphasizes the difference between:

«Event Detection»

and

«Behavioral Detection»

A mature detection capability should not only ask:

"Did an event occur?"

It should also ask:

"Does this sequence of events make sense for this identity, device, user, and workflow?"

---

Defensive Correlation Model

A simplified analytical model for the scenario is:

Identity Event
      ↓
User Interaction
      ↓
Application / Web Event
      ↓
Authentication Context
      ↓
Security Telemetry
      ↓
Correlation
      ↓
Investigation
      ↓
Response

The goal is not necessarily to generate an alert for every individual event.

Instead, the goal is to identify meaningful combinations of weak signals while minimizing unnecessary alerts.

This is where detection engineering becomes particularly important.

---

Why Correlation Matters

Without correlation, an analyst may see several unrelated events:

- A normal authentication.
- A normal email.
- A normal browser request.
- A normal application event.

With sufficient context, however, the same events may form a suspicious sequence.

This creates an important defensive principle:

«The security value of an event is often determined by its context, not by the event alone.»

11. Assessment Perspective

The exercise demonstrates that identity security cannot be evaluated solely by asking whether authentication controls function correctly.

A more complete assessment considers:

Identity
   +
Trust
   +
User Behavior
   +
Application Context
   +
Telemetry
   +
Detection
   +
Response

The purpose of the simulation was therefore not to prove that a specific technical control could be bypassed.

Instead, it was to examine how the surrounding security ecosystem behaves when multiple trust relationships are placed under controlled pressure.

---

12. Key Takeaway

The central lesson from Account Pressure is simple:

«Security controls may be individually effective while the workflow surrounding them still creates opportunities for abuse.»

A resilient organization should therefore be able to:

Recognize suspicious behavior.

Correlate seemingly unrelated events.

Investigate the complete sequence.

Respond proportionally.

Improve based on the evidence collected.

The assessment ultimately reinforces a broader principle of modern security:

«Trust should be continuously evaluated, not permanently assumed.»
